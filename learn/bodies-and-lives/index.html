In the FAQ: Who is Moonstrate for?

Bodies change over a lifetime, and no two are the same. This part covers both sides of that: the transitions your cycle moves through as you get older, and what periods mean for trans and nonbinary people. Most of what follows is normal variation, with only a few things worth flagging to a clinician.

First periods

Most people start their period around age 12 or 13, and nearly everyone by 15. The first couple of years are often irregular, with cycles that come at uneven intervals, and that's normal as the body settles into its rhythm, usually within about three years. In these early years cycles can range more widely than they will later. It's worth a check-in with a clinician if a period hasn't started by around age 15, or if there are no other signs of puberty by around 13.

Perimenopause and menopause

Often somewhere in your mid-40s, the cycle begins a years-long transition. The hallmark is rising variability: cycles that lengthen, shorten, skip, or change in flow. That unpredictability is itself the normal part. Menopause is reached when you've gone 12 months with no period at all, on average around age 51. Hot flushes and night sweats are common along the way. The clear flag here: any bleeding after menopause always deserves a check, and so does very heavy or unusually frequent bleeding during the transition. It's worth not simply assuming it's "just perimenopause."

After having a baby

The bleeding right after birth (called lochia) isn't a period. It's your body healing, and it tapers off over a few weeks before periods return. When your period actually comes back varies a lot, and breastfeeding tends to delay it, sometimes by many months. One important thing: ovulation can return before your first period does, so you can become fertile again without warning. That means cycle tracking isn't reliable as birth control in this stretch. Breastfeeding can offer real protection, but only while all three of these are true at once: you're breastfeeding exclusively, your baby is under six months, and your period hasn't returned. Once any of those changes, it stops being reliable.

For trans and nonbinary bodies

Not everyone who menstruates is a woman, and this resource is for all of you. Trans men, nonbinary people, and anyone with a uterus can have a cycle, and none of it says anything about your gender.

If you take testosterone for gender-affirming care, it usually stops your periods within several months, though some bleeding can still happen, especially early on or if a dose is missed. One thing that's genuinely important to know: testosterone is not birth control. Your periods stopping does not mean you've stopped ovulating, and pregnancy is still possible, so if that matters for you it's worth talking to a clinician about contraception.

Periods can be a real source of dysphoria, and there are ways to reduce or stop them that you can talk through with a clinician. Whatever you need, tracking is here to help you understand your body on your own terms.
