What's normal discharge, and what isn't

Discharge is normal and healthy. It's how your vagina keeps itself clean and protected, and it changes across your cycle: drier and stickier after your period, then clear, slippery, and stretchy like egg white around ovulation, then thicker again. Clear or white is the usual range, and a mild scent of your own is normal too. The amount varies from person to person and day to day. What's worth noticing is a change from what's usual for you, like a new strong or unpleasant smell, or a different color or texture.

Your vagina cleans itself, so there's no need to wash inside it. Douching actually does harm by upsetting the natural balance and raising your risk of infection. Gentle washing on the outside with water, or a plain, fragrance-free soap if you like, is all you need.

A few changes are worth getting checked, not to diagnose yourself but because a clinician can sort them out quickly: a gray discharge with a fishy smell, a thick white one with itching, or a green, yellow, or frothy discharge. Itching, burning, soreness, or pain when you pee are worth a look too, whatever the color.

Behind all of this is your vaginal microbiome, a community of good bacteria (mostly lactobacilli) that keeps things slightly acidic and protects against infection. It naturally shifts a little across your cycle, and things like antibiotics, semen, or douching can throw it off balance for a while. That's also why bacterial vaginosis (BV), one of the most common causes of unusual discharge, is an imbalance in that ecosystem rather than a sign of being unclean, and why it often comes back even after treatment. It's common and treatable, and it's not a sign you did anything wrong. If you think you might have it, it's worth seeing a clinician rather than waiting it out. BV is treated with prescription antibiotics, and treatment is recommended whenever it's causing symptoms, partly because untreated BV can make some other infections, including sexually transmitted infections (STIs), easier to catch. It's also easy to mistake for a yeast infection, and pharmacy yeast treatments won't clear it, so a quick check is worth it.

One last thing, because the internet is full of it: your vagina does not need detoxing, steaming, scented washes, or special cleansing products. Those don't help and can make things worse. Probiotics and boric acid come up a lot too. The evidence for probiotics is thin, and boric acid has a real but narrow medical use, so it's worth asking a clinician rather than self-prescribing, and never swallowing it. The simplest routine is usually the healthiest one.

Your gut and your period

If your bathroom habits change around your period, you're not imagining it. The same natural compounds that cause cramps, called prostaglandins, also stir up your gut, which is why looser or more frequent stools are common in the first day or two. Some people get the opposite in the days before, feeling more constipated as progesterone slows things down, then looser once bleeding starts. If you have irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), you may notice it flares around this time. All of this is common and usually settles within a few days. It's worth mentioning to a clinician if it's severe, or if you ever see blood in your stool.

Sex and your cycle

Desire can shift across your cycle, and some people notice more of it around ovulation. But it's a loose tendency at most. It varies a lot from person to person, and plenty of people notice no pattern at all, so there's nothing wrong if yours doesn't follow a script.

Sex during your period is completely safe and normal, and for some people orgasms even ease cramps. Two things worth remembering: you can still get pregnant during your period, and a period offers no protection from STIs, so whatever you'd normally use still applies.

Natural lubrication changes with your hormones too, and dryness is common at certain points in your cycle, on some birth control, and around menopause. It's physical, not a sign of how into it you are, and lubricant helps.

Sex that hurts is worth getting checked. Painful sex is common, but that doesn't mean you have to put up with it. It can come from dryness, tension, an infection, or conditions like endometriosis, and a clinician can help.

Why your cycle changed this month

If a period turns up late, early, or just different one month, the reason is usually something ordinary, and it usually sorts itself out within a cycle or two. A stressful stretch is one of the most common: stress can quietly push back the timing of ovulation, and the period follows. Being unwell, especially with a fever, can do the same, and so can a big change to your sleep, a run of night shifts, or crossing several time zones.

Some medications shift your cycle too. Certain mental-health medicines, some epilepsy medicines, strong pain medicines like opioids, thyroid treatment, and blood thinners can all change your timing or flow, and a few medicines, such as some treatments for endometriosis, are meant to pause your cycle on purpose. So a new prescription is worth bearing in mind, and worth raising with whoever prescribed it rather than stopping anything on your own.

A recent vaccine can nudge things as well. This looks like a general response to your immune system being switched on rather than anything specific to one shot. COVID-19 vaccination has been linked to a small, temporary change in cycle length that settles back within a cycle or so, and the flu vaccine shows a similar tiny blip, with no effect on your flow or your fertility.

A couple of reassuring notes, since these worries are common: ordinary antibiotics don't disrupt your natural cycle, and the HPV (human papillomavirus) vaccine has not been linked to ovarian problems, despite claims you may have come across.

The one cause worth keeping on your radar is your thyroid, because when it's over- or under-active it tends to change periods in a way that sticks around rather than passing. That's really the whole rule of thumb here: a single odd cycle is almost always fine, but a change that keeps up, cycles that regularly run shorter than 21 days or longer than 35, or no period for three months or more, is worth a conversation with a clinician.
