When something's worth checking

From Learn about your cycle. General wellness information, not medical advice. Sources.

Sources for this page: Conditions awareness · Conditions that fluctuate with the cycle · Mental health across the cycle · Energy availability, body composition & the cycle · Bleeding, variability & prediction · Period pain

In the FAQ: Is it medical advice, or a contraceptive?

Most cycle variation is completely normal, and this section isn't meant to make you anxious. If anything it's meant to do the reverse. Knowing the few things genuinely worth raising means you can relax about everything else. None of this is a diagnosis. It's a short list of "worth a conversation."

Signs worth mentioning to a clinician

  • A cycle that's regularly shorter than 21 days or longer than 35.
  • Cycle lengths that swing by more than about a week over a year.
  • Bleeding that lasts longer than 7 days.
  • Soaking through a pad or tampon every hour, or passing large clots.
  • No period for 90 days or more, when you're not pregnant, breastfeeding, or through menopause.
  • Bleeding between periods, after sex, or any bleeding after menopause.

None of these means something is wrong. They're just worth sharing, because they're the kind of thing a clinician would want to know.

A missing or irregular period isn't always about fitness

If you're very active and your period gets lighter, irregular, or stops, that can be a sign you're not eating enough to match what you're doing, rather than a sign of being fit. It's worth paying attention to, and worth a conversation. Your cycle is one of the ways your body tells you it has enough to work with. When you're doing a lot and not taking in enough to match it, your body may not have the spare energy to run a cycle, and fueling more consistently often helps it come back.

How your body's energy shows up in your cycle

Your body keeps track of how much energy it has to spare, from what you eat compared with what you use, and it runs the full cycle when there's enough. So big or sudden changes can unsettle your cycle for a while: eating a lot less than usual, training a lot harder, being ill, or your body changing quickly in either direction. What your body is looking for is enough energy, steadily. Fat tissue is part of this picture too. It isn't just storage; it's hormonally active and makes some of its own estrogen, so it works like a signal. Higher levels can be linked with cycles becoming less predictable or bleeding heavier, though how it plays out varies a lot. If your cycles are persistently off, a clinician can help you read the pattern.

One related thing worth knowing: going many months with no period at all means the lining isn't getting its regular reset, and over the long run that's worth a clinician's eyes. It isn't an emergency, just a good reason to check in.

Mental health across your cycle

If you already live with something like depression, anxiety, or bipolar, you might notice it gets harder around the time your period starts, then eases over its first few days. This is real, and it has a name: premenstrual exacerbation. It can sound like PMS, and it comes from the same hormone drop, but the difference is what's there the rest of the month. PMS, and its more severe form PMDD, are symptoms that show up before your period and clear once it's underway. Premenstrual exacerbation is a condition you live with all month that gets worse at that point. The difference matters, because a flare like this can look like a treatment that isn't working when it may be your cycle. It comes down to how sensitive your system is to a normal hormone change, not anything being broken.

Two things worth saying plainly. First, if your low or dark days cluster around that part of your cycle, logging them can help you and a clinician see the pattern, and there are treatments that help, so it's worth support rather than something to ride out alone. Second, for some people this window is when thoughts of self-harm or suicide get stronger. If that's you, please reach out. Talk to a clinician, or a crisis line right now if it feels urgent. In the US you can call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, free and confidential, any time of day. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com will point you to a line near you. It's a real and known pattern, and help is available.

Conditions that can flare with your cycle

Your cycle can tug on other health conditions, not just your period itself. Migraine is the common one: a lot of people get migraines in the day or two before their period, when estrogen drops, and these can run longer or hit harder than usual. Some people with epilepsy have more seizures around their period, and some people with asthma find it harder to manage then. If any of these are part of your life, noting when the flares happen can help you and your clinician spot the link, which is genuinely useful.

Heavy periods deserve a mention here too. Losing a lot of blood month after month is one of the main causes of low iron, which can leave you drained or short of breath without an obvious reason. If your periods are heavy and you're often exhausted, it's worth getting your iron checked, because it's very treatable.

When period pain or symptoms are more than "normal"

Heavy, severely painful, or life-disrupting periods are common. But common doesn't mean healthy, and it isn't something you just have to endure. Effective help exists, and it's worth asking for.

One pattern is worth knowing on its own. Period pain that starts a few days before bleeding, gets worse as your period goes on, doesn't go away when it ends, or gets worse over the years can point to an underlying cause, and it's worth raising with a clinician.

Here are a few things these conversations are sometimes about:

  • Endometriosis, where tissue like the uterine lining grows outside the uterus. It can cause period pain severe enough to limit daily life, or pain with sex, bowel movements, or urination. It affects around one in ten menstruators, and it's often diagnosed years later than it should be, which is exactly why raising it early matters.
  • PMOS (polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome), which until 2026 was called PCOS, or polycystic ovary syndrome. It's a common hormonal condition and a frequent reason for irregular or absent periods. The old name suggested cysts on the ovaries, which was misleading, and part of why it was renamed. It happens at every body size ("lean PMOS" is real), insulin's signaling is usually part of the picture, and a tracker doesn't diagnose it. If cycles are persistently irregular, a clinician can assess, and good care is weight-neutral.
  • Fibroids or adenomyosis, which can bring very heavy or painful periods and a feeling of pelvic pressure.
  • PMDD, when premenstrual mood symptoms are severe or disabling rather than just noticeable. It's treatable, and you don't have to white-knuckle through it.

Ovarian cysts are common and usually harmless. The most common kind forms as part of a normal cycle, from a follicle or the corpus luteum (see How your cycle works), usually causes no symptoms, and often goes away on its own within a couple of months. Other kinds can form with endometriosis or grow for other reasons, and most of those aren't cancer either. The one to act on quickly: sudden, severe pain low in your belly, especially with nausea or vomiting, can mean a cyst has burst or twisted the ovary, and it needs urgent care.

You don't need to diagnose yourself from this list. The point is simpler: if any of it sounds like your experience, it's a reasonable thing to bring to someone who can help.
